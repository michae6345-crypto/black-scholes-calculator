1. Setup and assumptions

The model needs a short list of things to be true. None of them are, exactly, but the price you get is still the market's common language.

  • The spot price \(S\) follows a lognormal geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility \(\sigma\).
  • The risk-free rate \(r\) and the continuous dividend yield \(q\) are constant.
  • Trading is continuous, in any size, with no transaction costs, taxes or short-sale limits.
  • The option is European: it can only be exercised at expiry \(T\).
  • \(N(\cdot)\) is the standard normal CDF and \(\phi(\cdot)\) its density. \(t\) is calendar time, so \(\partial_t\) means "as time passes".

2. The underlying

Under the real-world measure the stock drifts at some \(\mu\) and pays out \(q\):

$$dS = (\mu - q)\,S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW .$$

Itô's lemma on \(\ln S\) turns the multiplicative noise into additive noise, and picks up the \(-\tfrac12\sigma^2\) correction:

$$d\ln S = \left(\mu - q - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma\,dW .$$

Pricing replaces \(\mu\) with \(r\) (the risk-neutral measure), so the terminal price is lognormal with a known mean and variance:

$$S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left(\left(r - q - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right),\qquad Z \sim N(0,1).$$

This is the line the Monte Carlo and the sample-path drawings use directly.

3. The hedged portfolio and the PDE

Hold one option \(V(S,t)\) and short \(\Delta\) shares. Itô on \(V\) gives its change over \(dt\):

$$dV = \partial_t V\,dt + \partial_S V\,dS + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V\,dt .$$

The portfolio \(\Pi = V - \Delta S\) (the short stock also owes the dividend \(q\Delta S\,dt\)) moves by

$$d\Pi = dV - \Delta\,dS - q\Delta S\,dt .$$

Choosing \(\Delta = \partial_S V\) kills the \(dW\) term, so \(\Pi\) is riskless over \(dt\) and must earn \(r\Pi\,dt\). Equating the two:

$$\partial_t V + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V + (r - q)\,S\,\partial_S V - rV = 0 .$$

Note \(\mu\) has dropped out. The equation is solved backwards from the payoff at \(T\):

$$C(S,T) = \max(S-K,0),\qquad P(S,T) = \max(K-S,0),$$ $$C(0,t)=0,\quad C(S,t)\to S e^{-q(T-t)} - K e^{-r(T-t)}\ \text{as}\ S\to\infty,$$ $$P(0,t)=K e^{-r(T-t)},\quad P(S,t)\to 0\ \text{as}\ S\to\infty .$$

4. Closed-form solution

With \(T\) now meaning time left to expiry, define

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r - q + \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}.$$

Then

$$C = S e^{-qT} N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2),$$ $$P = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S e^{-qT} N(-d_1).$$

\(N(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money, and \(e^{-qT}N(d_1)\) is the call's delta. When \(T=0\) or \(\sigma=0\) the code returns the discounted intrinsic value \(\max(\pm(Se^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}),0)\) and zero for every Greek.

5. Put–call parity

A long call and a short put with the same strike is a forward, so no model is needed for this one:

$$C - P = S e^{-qT} - K e^{-rT}.$$

Differentiating it is a quick check on the Greek tables below: \(\Delta_C - \Delta_P = e^{-qT}\), and gamma, vega, vanna, vomma, veta, speed, zomma, color and ultima are identical for the call and the put.

9. Implied volatility

Given a market price \(V_{mkt}\), find the \(\sigma\) that the formula maps to it. Newton's method uses vega as the slope:

$$\sigma_{n+1} = \sigma_n - \frac{V(\sigma_n) - V_{mkt}}{\nu(\sigma_n)} .$$

The starting guess is the Brenner–Subrahmanyam approximation \(\sigma_0 \approx \sqrt{2\pi/T}\;V_{mkt}/S\). Deep in or out of the money vega goes to zero, so a Newton step can shoot off to a nonsense value. The code keeps a bracket \([\sigma_{lo},\sigma_{hi}]\) that always contains the root, tightens it after every evaluation, and falls back to the bisection midpoint whenever the Newton step lands outside it. It stops when \(|V(\sigma_n) - V_{mkt}| < 10^{-8}\) or after 60 iterations, and refuses prices below intrinsic or above what \(\sigma = 500\%\) can produce.

10. Binomial tree (CRR)

Split \(T\) into \(n\) steps of \(\Delta t = T/n\). Each step the price goes up by \(u\) or down by \(d\):

$$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad d = \frac{1}{u},\qquad p = \frac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t} - d}{u - d} .$$

Fill the last column with payoffs and roll back one step at a time, discounting the risk-neutral expectation:

$$V_{n,i} = \max(\pm(S u^i d^{\,n-i} - K), 0),\qquad V_{k,i} = e^{-r\Delta t}\left(p\,V_{k+1,i+1} + (1-p)\,V_{k+1,i}\right).$$

For an American option compare with immediate exercise at every node:

$$V_{k,i} = \max\!\left(e^{-r\Delta t}\left(p\,V_{k+1,i+1} + (1-p)\,V_{k+1,i}\right),\ \text{payoff}(S u^i d^{\,k-i})\right).$$

The tree's log-returns have the right mean and variance to first order in \(\Delta t\), so as \(\Delta t \to 0\) the European price converges to Black–Scholes, with an oscillating error that shrinks like \(1/n\).

11. Finite differences

Work in time to expiry \(\tau = T - t\), so the PDE runs forward from the payoff. Put the grid on \(S_j = j\,\Delta S\) for \(j = 0,\dots,M\) with \(S_{max} = 4\max(S,K)\), and write \(V_j^n\) for the value at \(S_j\) and \(\tau_n = n\,\Delta\tau\). Central differences in \(S\) give the operator

$$\mathcal{L}V_j = \tfrac12\sigma^2 j^2\,(V_{j+1} - 2V_j + V_{j-1}) + \tfrac12(r-q)\,j\,(V_{j+1} - V_{j-1}) - rV_j .$$

Crank–Nicolson averages it between the old and new time levels, \(V^{n+1} - V^n = \tfrac12\Delta\tau\,(\mathcal{L}V^{n+1} + \mathcal{L}V^n)\). Collecting terms with

$$a_j = \tfrac14\Delta\tau\left(\sigma^2 j^2 - (r-q)j\right),\quad b_j = -\tfrac12\Delta\tau\left(\sigma^2 j^2 + r\right),\quad c_j = \tfrac14\Delta\tau\left(\sigma^2 j^2 + (r-q)j\right)$$

gives one tridiagonal system per time step:

$$-a_j V_{j-1}^{n+1} + (1 - b_j)\,V_j^{n+1} - c_j V_{j+1}^{n+1} = a_j V_{j-1}^{n} + (1 + b_j)\,V_j^{n} + c_j V_{j+1}^{n}.$$

The Thomas algorithm solves it in \(O(M)\). The boundaries are the closed-form limits from section 3, so they are exact at every step:

$$C_0^n = 0,\quad C_M^n = S_{max}e^{-q\tau_n} - Ke^{-r\tau_n},\qquad P_0^n = Ke^{-r\tau_n},\quad P_M^n = 0 .$$

The price at the input spot is a linear interpolation between the two grid nodes around it. The scheme is second-order in both \(\Delta S\) and \(\Delta\tau\) and unconditionally stable.

12. Monte Carlo

Draw \(Z_i \sim N(0,1)\), map each to a terminal price with the formula from section 2, and average the discounted payoffs:

$$\hat V = \frac{e^{-rT}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{payoff}(S_T^{(i)}) .$$

The code uses antithetic variates: every draw \(Z\) is paired with \(-Z\), and the pair's average payoff is one sample. The two are negatively correlated, so the variance of each sample drops for free:

$$X_i = \tfrac12 e^{-rT}\left(\text{payoff}(S_T(Z_i)) + \text{payoff}(S_T(-Z_i))\right),\qquad \hat V = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2} X_i .$$

The standard error is the sample standard deviation of the \(X_i\) over the square root of their count, so it shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Four times the paths buys one more bit of precision.

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{n/2}} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} .$$

13. Probabilities

These are the numbers on the analytics page. All are under the risk-neutral measure, so they are the probabilities the price implies, not a forecast.

Finishing above the strike:

$$P(S_T > K) = N(d_2),\qquad P(S_T < K) = N(-d_2).$$

The one-standard-deviation expected move, in price units:

$$\text{expected move} = S\,\sigma\sqrt{T}.$$

The probability of touching the strike at any time before expiry, from the reflection principle for a driftless Brownian motion:

$$P(\text{touch}) = 2\,N\!\left(-\frac{\left|\ln(K/S)\right|}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right).$$

This ignores the drift \(r - q - \tfrac12\sigma^2\), which is a fair approximation when \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) is large relative to it. It is roughly twice the probability of finishing beyond the strike.

The density of \(S_T\) is lognormal. With

$$\mu = \ln S + \left(r - q - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T,$$

it is

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{x\,\sigma\sqrt{T}}\,\phi\!\left(\frac{\ln x - \mu}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right),\qquad x > 0 .$$

Its median is \(e^{\mu}\), its mean is the forward \(S e^{(r-q)T}\), and the 68% and 95% ranges are \(e^{\mu \pm z\,\sigma\sqrt{T}}\) with \(z\) the matching normal quantile.

These formulas match assets/bs.js and the Python package line for line, including the sign conventions: theta, charm, color and veta are derivatives with respect to calendar time moving forward, and vanna, vomma, zomma and ultima are per unit of \(\sigma\) before the quoting scale is applied.
